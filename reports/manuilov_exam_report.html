Assignment: Final exam — TV program recommendation on sweet.tv (Kaggle sweettv-tv-program-recommender)

Abstract — The task is to predict, for 1,260 test users, the five TV programmes they will watch in weeks 31-42 from a view log of weeks 11-30 and the broadcast schedule, in which only 10.8% of the airings of weeks 11-20 carry a programme id. I join views to airings by channel and interval overlap, count a programme as watched when one continuous view covers more than 50% of an airing, compare popularity, implicit ALS, content-based filtering, repeat models and a slot-habit model on one temporal hold-out, and re-rank their candidates with a LightGBM ensemble. The designated final submission scores 0.44493 private / 0.46071 public MAP@5 against 0.24426 / 0.25483 for popularity; on validation it reaches 0.4265 against 0.4080 for the best single model. Takeaway: the definition of the label mattered more than the model; my first, any-overlap label fitted three leaderboard points almost perfectly and was still wrong.

Index Terms — TV recommendation, implicit feedback, learning to rank, temporal validation, MAP@5

1. Introduction

Problem statement. sweet.tv published a view log (5,821,987 views of 4,914 users, 2020-03-09 to 2020-07-26) and three schedule files (weeks 11-20, 21-30, 31-42) [1]. A view has a user, a channel and a time interval; a programme exists only in the schedule. For each of the 1,260 users in submission.csv the system must output five tv_show_ids for weeks 31-42; the metric is MAP@5. Only labelled airings can become items: 10.76% of the schedule rows of weeks 11-20 have tv_show_id ≠ 0 (11.31% and 11.18% in the other two files, docs/data_inspection.md).

Motivation. Linear TV is watched by habit: the same news, series and slots, day after day. A recommender that only re-lists what a user already watches can still be useful, but it has to be measured on the right notion of "watched", and a model has to be judged on future weeks it has not seen.

Concrete example. Test user 2725163102947201526 watched 662.4 hours and touched 504 labelled programmes in weeks 11-20. For weeks 21-30 the final setup recommends Сегодня, Факты, Утро с Украиной, Викна-Новости and ТСН; two of them (Сегодня, Утро с Украиной) are in the user's true top-5, which gives AP@5 = 0.3333 (reports/hand_check.md).

Contributions.

  • A reproducible pipeline from raw CSVs to a ranked list, with a checked join and a coverage-based label, and an honest record of how a wrong validation label was detected with one diagnostic submission (§5).
  • A comparison of six single methods and two hybrids on the same hold-out, with a temporal check for every learned re-ranker, and a final hybrid that improves private MAP@5 from 0.40026 (best single-model submission) to 0.44493.

2. Related Work

Prior work consulted. The competition page [1] hosts the data and the leaderboard. A prize-winning write-up for this competition [2] counts a programme as watched only when more than 80% of it was seen without switching channels, uses a simple count-based baseline ("more than 50% of the show in a single sitting") and a second layer with LightGBM; I took the label idea and some feature ideas from it, no code. Implicit ALS [3] weights observed interactions by confidence instead of treating them as ratings; I used the implicit library [7]. The re-ranker is gradient boosting with LightGBM [4] and the LambdaRank objective [5], which optimizes a ranking metric directly. Cremonesi et al. [6] showed that a non-personalized popularity list is a strong top-N baseline, which is why every method here is compared with one.

Alternatives considered.

  • Association rules (not implemented, discussed only conceptually): rules such as "watches A ⇒ watches B" capture co-viewing, but here the strongest signal is repeating the same programme, which a count already captures.
  • Personalized PageRank on a co-view graph (not implemented, conceptual): diffusion spreads a user's history to neighbouring programmes, i.e. towards new items; on this data even ALS, which also generalizes from co-viewing, stayed below popularity (Table I).
  • LightFM, used as a candidate source in [2]: not tried for lack of time; ALS and CBF suggested that similarity-based candidates add little here.

3. Method

Approach. Everything runs offline in Python (src/, one module per step, python -m src.run_all reproduces the tables, examples and figures). A temporal hold-out mirrors the real task: history weeks 11-20, target weeks 21-30. The final model is a second layer over candidates from simple habit-based models.

Pipeline.

  1. Join (build_interactions.py): glue the three schedules (3,441,607 airings kept, end = start + duration in seconds); merge overlapping or touching views of the same user on the same channel (5,821,987 views → 5,441,773 intervals, viewing time −1.3%), so parallel devices do not count twice; split intervals at Monday 00:00 so every interaction belongs to one ISO week; join on vsetv_id = channel_id with interval overlap. Result: 3,026,937 labelled (user, airing, week) pairs; 52.89% of viewing time falls on labelled airings, 41.15% on tv_show_id = 0, 5.96% matches no airing.
  2. Label (coverage.py): for every (user, airing) the share of the airing covered by the best single continuous view. Fig. 1 shows why a threshold is needed: most pairs are short glimpses, a second peak are complete airings. A programme's target count = airings with coverage > 50% (cov50); the user's top-5 by that count is the validation ground truth.
  3. Candidates: top-50 per user from seven sources: repeat_cov (with and without its minimum-count filter), the > 30% coverage ranking, the earlier loyalty repeat, popularity, ALS and slot habits; 121 candidates per user contain 83.9% of the true top-5 (docs/hybrid_v4_results.md).
  4. Features (53): coverage counts at 30/50/80% and their ranks, decayed days/airings/seconds, weeks since the last full watch, loyalty, ALS score and rank, CBF cosine, slot score and rank, share of the user's time on the programme's channel, time-of-day fit, airings in the target window and their change against the history, popularity, category and channel.
  5. Re-ranker: LightGBM LambdaRank (graded by log2 of the count) and binary models, three seeds each, rank-averaged; trained on five sliding folds (11-17→18-20 ... 11-27→28-30) with recency weights and cov80 labels (§3, decisions).
  6. Top-5: the five best-scored candidates; if fewer than five exist, the shared popularity fallback fills the list.

Fig. 1. Left: coverage of labelled airings by one continuous view (3,014,129 (user, airing) pairs, weeks 11-30; > 30 / 50 / 80%: 39.9 / 29.2 / 19.0% of pairs). Right: public and private score of all ten Kaggle submissions (Table II).

Tools & libraries. Python 3.11.9 on Windows 11 (Intel i7-11370H, 4 cores, 15.7 GB RAM); DuckDB 2.0.0 for joins [9]; pandas 3.1.0, NumPy 2.4.6, SciPy 1.17.1; implicit 0.7.3 [7]; LightGBM 4.7.0 [8]; scikit-learn 1.9.1 (TF-IDF); matplotlib 3.11.2. Pinned versions: requirements.txt.

Key design decisions.

  • A coverage label, chosen from the leaderboard, not from validation. Validation can only rank models against a label I define; which label matches the hidden one can only be tested with submissions that disagree about it (§5). cov50 fits the four discriminating leaderboard points best of the candidates I tried; the final ranker is trained on cov80 labels, which the write-up names [2].
  • A strict reading of the test-schedule rule. The weeks 31-42 schedule is used only for which programme airs on which channel and when (candidate pool, airing counts, slots). Content metadata comes only from schedules up to week 30, so test-only programmes have no CBF vector. No view after week 30 exists or is used.
  • Recency instead of more tuning. The final ranker is trained on five sliding folds, weighted 0.5(28 − first target week)/4 (0.18 ... 1.0), and refit on all users with the validated number of trees.

Configuration. Popularity: watch seconds, last 10 history weeks. ALS: BM25-weighted decayed days, 8 factors, regularization 0.01, α = 0.3, 15 iterations. CBF: TF-IDF over category, genres, decade, director and up to 5 actors per row (names in ≥ 2 programmes), people weight 4, user profile = weighted mean, cosine. repeat_cov: airings with > 30% coverage, half-life 4 weeks, loyalty scaling with β = 10, at least 3 such watches. Slot habits: channel × weekday/weekend × hour, half-life 8. LightGBM: learning rate 0.05, LambdaRank 15 leaves / 20 min. child samples, binary 63 / 100, subsample and column sample 0.8, early stopping (100 rounds) on 10% of training users. Seed 42 everywhere (ALS, LightGBM with deterministic=True, holdouts, samples); ensembles add 43 and 44. Runtime: src.run_all 1,397 s end to end, of which the final ensemble 1,263 s; every single method ≤ 2.3 s (docs/runtimes.md, Table I).

4. Experiments

Setup. Validation: history weeks 11-20, target weeks 21-30, the 1,260 test users (their weeks 21-30 views are training data, so they may be used). Candidate pool: the 6,376 labelled programmes airing in weeks 21-30, identical for every method; repeats of already-watched programmes are allowed. MAP@5 is the Kaggle formula (src/metrics.py); users with an empty ground truth count as 0. Every method outputs exactly five distinct ids: own ranking first, then the shared popularity fallback. Cold start: 10 test users have no labelled interaction in weeks 11-20 and 2 in weeks 11-30 (docs/join_summary.md); they get the fallback in the single methods. The hybrids never need it, because popularity is one of their candidate sources. A re-ranker's temporal check is the same model trained only on folds whose labels end before week 21. Hardware: as in §3.

Familiar vs new. For the heavy viewer every method except CBF recommends only programmes the user already watched; the user's true top-5, however, contains two children's series (Удивительный мир Гамбола, Время приключений) that no method proposed, possibly watched by another household member on the same account. For the light viewer repeat_cov has nothing of its own and falls back to popularity in all five slots, while the hybrid fills them from its own candidates and finds the one hit, ТСН, like popularity does.

How the methods differ. ALS and popularity both put the big news shows first: on this data a low-rank model mostly learns popularity (its best configuration has only 8 factors). CBF looks for similar metadata and proposes new entertainment shows, none of which the users watched. Repeat models know only the user's own history; slot habits know only the user's channels and hours. The re-ranker uses both: in the hybrid_v2 setup the slot rank carries 44.7% of the total gain and the repeat rank 15.1% (docs/hybrid_cov50_feature_importance.md). Association rules and personalized PageRank were not implemented; conceptually, rules would add "after A, B" co-viewing and PageRank would spread a history to neighbouring programmes, i.e. towards discovery, the direction in which ALS and CBF already did worse here.

Decision. For sweet.tv I would choose the two-layer strategy: habit-based candidates (repeat counts on coverage, slot habits, popularity) re-ranked by a learned model. It is the best on validation and on the leaderboard (Table II), and it degrades gracefully for thin histories. Its cost is the honest trade-off: repeat_cov runs in about 1 s and reaches 0.4080 on validation, the final ensemble needs about 21 minutes for 0.4265; a service could serve repeat_cov as the always-on default and refresh the re-ranker offline. For the user experience the lists are mostly familiar programmes; this matches how linear TV is watched, but it is not discovery, and nothing in the data tells whether a recommended new programme would have been enjoyed. Limitations: only 10.8% of airtime has an id, so the remaining viewing is invisible as items; the hidden label is still only approximated (§5); absence from the log does not mean a user never saw a programme elsewhere.

5. Discussion

[PAVEL: draft below, to be rewritten in my own words]

Failure case: a validation label that fitted the leaderboard and was wrong. I first counted a programme as watched on any overlap and ranked by distinct days (n_days). Three submissions (popularity, two repeat models) fitted this label almost perfectly: validation gaps 1.297 / 1.425 / 1.098 against leaderboard gaps 1.302 / 1.441 / 1.107, coefficient of variation of the LB/val ratio 0.0048 (docs/target_definition.md). I selected models on it for three stages.

Root cause. All three submissions ranked programmes by any-overlap counts, so they were bound to agree with an any-overlap label; the comparison could rank candidate labels only among similar models and could not test a definition none of them used. A diagnostic submission built on a different definition (repeat_cont50, > 50% continuous coverage) settled it: the any-overlap label predicted 0.298, the coverage labels 0.380-0.387, and it scored 0.417 public / 0.400 private. With four points, n_days became the worst candidate (cv 0.156) and cov50 the best (cv 0.057), though no candidate fits well: even for cov50 the ratio rises from 0.93 (popularity) to 1.09 (repeat_cont50).

Fix and verification. I switched selection to cov50, re-tuned repeat on it (0.3841 → 0.4080) and retrained the hybrid on coverage labels; the public score went from 0.36752 (repeat_v2) to 0.41706 (repeat_cont50) and 0.45010 (first hybrid on coverage labels). A second, smaller error: converting epoch seconds back to timestamps went through the DuckDB session time zone (Europe/Moscow), so all day counts started at 21:00; 2.1% of (user, programme, week) day counts changed after the fix (docs/errata.md). A third finding is a limitation: the LightGBM hybrids are not bit-for-bit reproducible (validation 0.4271 / 0.4260 / 0.4263 for the hybrid_v2 setup in three runs; a regenerated final.csv matches the scored one in 824 of 1,260 rows, 96.9% of ids). The likely cause, not verified, is tie-breaking in rank features on rows whose order DuckDB does not fix.

What worked.

  • A cheap diagnostic submission whose outcome I wrote down in advance; it was worth more than the whole label study.
  • Checking the join against a brute-force version before building anything on it.
  • The temporal check: in the first hybrid the binary model looked best but lost most when trained only on earlier weeks (0.4660 → 0.4521 vs 0.4656 → 0.4581 for LambdaRank, any-overlap label, docs/hybrid_results.md), so I used LambdaRank.

What surprised me or did not work.

  • Both required methods stayed below popularity (ALS 0.2528, CBF 0.1154 vs 0.2752): on linear TV, habit beats similarity.
  • Slot habits are strong alone (0.4015) but added little on top of repeat in the hybrid.
  • Recency (five weighted folds) was invisible on validation (0.4270 vs 0.4272) but every later refit, which also had more candidates and features, scored 0.007-0.012 higher on the private leaderboard than hybrid_v2 (0.43259); the data cannot separate the share of recency.

Next improvement. Make the rank features deterministic (sort before ranking) and re-run the final setup twice; only then compare variants that differ by less than 0.002.
